Domain Randomization Best Practices:

  - Get a policy working with ZERO randomization first. DR widens a working
    basin; it cannot create one, and it hides which change broke training.
  - Randomize what you measured failing, not everything you can. Each lever
    should trace to a symptom (sway -> COM/IMU bias, chatter -> latency+rate)
    or a known model gap; blanket DR just makes the task harder to learn.
  - Per-episode constants and per-step noise are different tools. Noise teaches
    filtering; constant biases (COM, IMU trim, gains, latency, payload) teach
    estimation and adaptation. Most sim-to-real error is a constant, not noise.
  - Derive ranges from measured hardware error with margin, not vibes: a
    measured ~2 deg trim becomes +-2.5 deg trained. Too narrow does nothing;
    too wide taxes learning for robustness you'll never need.
  - Train wider than you deploy. Behavior degrades at the edge of the training
    distribution, so the whole deployed envelope must sit in the interior.
  - A regime not sampled is a regime not learned. Check the command/schedule
    distribution covers standing, transitions through zero, direct-rate control
    - whatever the operator will actually do - not just the steady cases.
  - Command realism is DR too: resample fast enough that each episode sees
    several transitions, and shape them like the real input device (ramps,
    wind-downs), or the policy meets its first real transition on hardware.
  - The real control chain is never instantaneous. Randomize 0-1 control steps
    of action delay plus first-order lag instead of modeling exact latency;
    exact models are wrong, tolerance to a range is robust.
  - Widen DR specifically where the model is known-wrong (parallel-mechanism
    ankles: +-50% there vs +-15% global). Uniform ranges assume uniform model
    quality, which is never true.
  - Randomize identically in every pipeline stage. A student distilled or
    finetuned in cleaner dynamics than its teacher trained in silently loses
    the robustness you paid for.
  - Never randomize the contract. Obs/action dimensions, order, and scaling
    stay fixed; randomize dynamics and inputs behind the interface, so the
    deploy stack survives every retrain.
  - Prove each lever reaches the physics, per env. Read back the solver-side
    values (masses, gains, COM) and check cross-env variance after a step - a
    lever that only writes a config buffer is a silent no-op, and the run will
    happily "succeed" without it.
  - Know each lever's reset semantics: some writes are startup-only (CPU-side
    gain writes), some must re-randomize from STORED DEFAULTS on reset - an
    additive mass event that re-adds every episode accumulates to nonsense.
  - Build rotations and biases through the framework's own math helpers. A
    hand-built identity quaternion in the wrong component convention is a
    180-degree flip that no error message will ever report.
  - DR slows convergence; raise the iteration budget and judge by the curves
    (failure rate, tracking error still falling), not by a fixed count.
  - Gate every retrain on the same fixed eval suite, with the previous
    policy's numbers as the baseline to beat. If the retrain doesn't move the
    measured symptoms, the new levers didn't do what you think.
  - Cap disturbances at what the ALLOWED recovery strategy can physically
    absorb. Feet-planted balance captures ~0.3 m/s; train it against 1.5 m/s
    shoves and RL will correctly invent stepping or a wide stance - the policy
    solved the task you posed, not the one you meant.
  - Prefer bounded force pulses over velocity kicks for contact-rich scenarios:
    forces are resistible by posture and resemble real manipulation reactions;
    velocity kicks teleport momentum into the root and mandate stepping.
